# Realtime research: how Google Slides does it, and the options including peer to peer

Research note for the realtime round, written 2026-10-01 against the worktree `Turboslide-realtime` at `f50b209d` (origin/main). It answers Kevin's question of 2026-10-01: "fix the syncing and people on the same slide issues. redesign or smthg. should it be peer to peer? check how google slides does it". Section 8 answers the peer to peer question in one paragraph and names the second best option. Every claim about the code cites a file and line of this tree. Every claim about a product cites the URL it was read from, on 2026-10-01. Prices carry that date. Latency figures in section 5 are estimates from round trip times and the code's own timers, not measurements; the measured numbers of production are in section 2.

## 1. Scope

Four options are compared for a TanStack Start app on Vercel fluid compute: (a) a server ordered log with a pub/sub fan out, (b) a stateful realtime host beside Vercel, (c) a CRDT with a server for persistence and awareness, (d) peer to peer over WebRTC. For each: the latency a keystroke and a caret would see between two browsers in one region and across regions, the cost at 50 and at 500 editor hours a day, what it means for this codebase, the lock in and the failure modes. Section 6 states the load model behind every dollar figure.

## 2. What production runs today

Production is `TURBOSLIDE_STORE=blob`, `TURBOSLIDE_REALTIME=blob`, `TURBOSLIDE_AUTHORIZE=shadow`, `TURBOSLIDE_MAIL=off`, no `DATABASE_URL`, no `REDIS_URL` (`docs/PEOPLE.md` line 13). The tier selection is `packages/realtime/src/select.ts` lines 1 to 10: an explicit `TURBOSLIDE_REALTIME` wins, `REDIS_URL` selects redis, `VERCEL` without Redis selects blob, otherwise memory. The blob tier's title row sentence is "Live cursors need a Redis store on this deployment" (`select.ts` line 23, `BLOB_TIER_NOTICE`).

The write path. A tab posts a batch of operations against its `base.seq` to `POST /api/decks/:id/ops` (`apps/studio/src/routes/api/decks.$deckId.ops.ts` lines 23 to 29): at most 64 entries and 256 kB, then the transform, the reducer, the validator and the compare and append. On the blob tier the admission is `admitOnBlob` (`apps/studio/src/server/room.ts` line 2094): the entry is transformed past every record that landed since its base (`transformEntry`, line 1485; the text transform is `packages/schema/src/transform.ts` lines 1 to 9, two primitive cases of client server operational transformation, every non text mutation last writer wins by server order), then written as a version record and committed by a conditional manifest put (`packages/store/src/blob-store.ts` lines 3 to 10: `deck.json` pushed with `ifMatch` on the version the instance synced, so two instances writing at once end with one commit and one conflict). Appends on one instance are spaced at least 1,000 ms apart (`packages/realtime/src/blob.ts` line 297, `minWriteSpacingMs`).

The read path. Each instance with an open stream polls the deck's pulse object every `HOSTED_POLL_MS` (2,000 ms, `packages/store/src/pulse.ts` line 30; 10,000 ms when a tab is alone, line 32) and reads the manifest, the presence record and the comments index only when the pulse moved (`blob.ts` lines 470 to 492). Presence is a shared record polled every 5 s and pushed at most every 5 s (`packages/store/src/presence-store.ts` lines 74 and 76). A tab holds one Server-Sent Events stream per instance (`apps/studio/src/routes/api/decks.$deckId.stream.ts` lines 33 to 34), capped at 256 streams per instance and 4 per anonymous identity (`packages/realtime/src/admission.ts` lines 25 to 41), and reopens on an 8 s gap (`packages/realtime/client/room-client.ts` line 230, `GAP_REOPEN_MS`). The client coalesces its own ops at 50 ms during a drag and 100 ms while typing, holds pending ops and transforms them against incoming ones, and undoes per author through `transformSince` (`room-client.ts` lines 2 to 10 and 410). Presence posts are batched at 80 ms and capped at 15 a second, with a 5 s heartbeat (`packages/realtime/src/protocol.ts` lines 54, 55 and 60).

The access side. The access record and the link grants are cached per instance for 5 s on the blob tier (`apps/studio/src/server/access.ts` lines 70 and 168, `BLOB_ACCESS_TTL_MS` and `LINK_GRANT_TTL_MS`), which is why a copied share link answers 404 to a second browser for up to 5 s. The agent writes over HTTP: `POST /api/actions/<action>` flushes the room first (`apps/studio/src/routes/api/actions.$action.ts` line 112, `flushRoom` of `room.ts` line 1088) and lands in the open tab through the stream, where `announceAgentWrite` (`apps/studio/src/editor/controller.tsx` lines 1660 to 1672) filters to entries whose author kind is `agent` from another client id, so a write under the person's own ids shows no banner.

The redis tier exists and has never run on production: one Redis stream per deck with `<seq>-0` ids, a head counter, a Lua compare and append, one pub/sub channel per deck that every instance with an open stream subscribes to once (`packages/realtime/src/redis.ts` lines 1 to 6; `packages/realtime/src/lua.ts` lines 1 to 17, `APPEND_SCRIPT`). The header of `redis.ts` says the Upstash REST API cannot block or subscribe, so the tier needs the Redis protocol over TCP (ioredis). Upstash's own REST documentation says SUBSCRIBE and PUBLISH are offered over Server-Sent Events while the blocking forms of XREAD, BLPOP and the sorted set pops are not (https://upstash.com/docs/redis/features/restapi), so the tier could also subscribe over REST if TCP is a problem on fluid compute.

Sign in exists in code and is off on production: better-auth with magic link, email OTP, device authorization and a GitHub provider behind `GITHUB_CLIENT_ID` and `GITHUB_CLIENT_SECRET` (`apps/studio/src/server/auth/better-auth.ts` lines 18 to 20, 32 to 33, 158 to 166, 233 to 261), all gated on a database.

The measured defects, from `docs/gslides-parity/polish/audit-collab.md` (Summary) and `docs/POLISH.md` section 6 (lines 128 and 129): two people typing in one title keep both words but the merge arrives 2.6 to 4.6 s later and neither sees the other's caret; a joiner's chip takes 2.5 to 10 s; save latency is 1.1 to 3.7 s; a colleague on a Viewer link never sees an edit without a reload; Follow is never offered to anonymous editors. `docs/SYNC.md` 6.1 names `sync.title.concurrent-both-kept` as broken at severity 3 and `sync.viewer.live-updates` as 0 of 30. `docs/SYNC.md` 4.3 puts the whole app at $102.58 a month at 50 editor hours a day and $614.64 at 500 on Pro, with provisioned memory the largest line ($73.25 and $335.03) and the 25 s session long poll most of it.

These numbers are the floor the blob tier cannot go under: a commit is at least one manifest put (hundreds of ms on Blob), the other instance learns of it on its next 2 s pulse tick, and presence moves on a 5 s record. The architecture, not a bug, sets the 2.6 to 4.6 s merge and the 2.5 to 10 s chip.

## 3. How Google Docs and Slides do it

Google's model is a central server that orders operations with operational transformation. The first party description is the Google Wave operational transformation whitepaper (https://svn.apache.org/repos/asf/incubator/wave/whitepapers/operational-transform/operational-transform.html): the server keeps "a single state space, which is the history of operations it has applied"; a client waits for the server's acknowledgement before sending more operations and "caches operations produced locally and sends them in bulk later"; the server has "only to transform the operation against the operation history, apply the transformed operation, and then broadcast it". The Docs team's own 2010 posts ("What's different about the new Google Docs: Conflict resolution", https://drive.googleblog.com/2010/09/whats-different-about-new-google-docs_22.html, and "Making collaboration fast", https://drive.googleblog.com/2010/09/whats-different-about-new-google-docs.html) exist but served only their title and navigation to the fetch on 2026-10-01, so they are cited as the source of the design and not quoted. Secondary descriptions agree on the mechanism: a cluster of stateful servers holds the in-memory state of each open document, transforms concurrent operations and broadcasts them, with a session cache mapping a document to its server and an append-only operation log for persistence (https://www.techinterview.org/post/3233463297/system-design-collaborative-document-editing-google-docs/?format=md; https://craft.do/blog/collaborative-editing; https://dev.to/dhanush___b/how-google-docs-uses-operational-transformation-for-real-time-collaboration-119). Presence (the coloured carets and the chips) travels on the same client to server channel; there is no peer to peer path.

The retired Google Realtime API was the same model offered as a library: "build collaborative apps using familiar JSON-based data models, while leaving the complexities of real-time synchronization to the API", with the data stored in Google Drive; it closed to new projects on 2017-11-28, went read only on 2018-12-11 and shut down on 2019-01-15, with Firestore as the suggested migration (https://workspaceupdates.googleblog.com/2017/11/committed-to-storage-apis-retiring.html).

The Slides API is the agent surface analogue. `presentations.batchUpdate` validates every request first, applies the batch atomically, and documents that "your changes may be altered with respect to collaborator changes", which is the server transforming an API write against concurrent edits; `WriteControl.requiredRevisionId` refuses a write against a stale revision with a 400 (https://developers.google.com/workspace/slides/api/reference/rest/v1/presentations/batchUpdate). That is the shape this codebase already has: `admitOnBlob` transforms a POST past what landed and answers "reload and rebase" when the base is too far behind (`room.ts` lines 2094 to 2106), and the agent's `deck.trash {id, baseRevision}` is a `requiredRevisionId`.

## 4. How Figma, Notion and Linear do it

Figma: "Figma clients are web pages that talk with a cluster of servers over WebSockets. Our servers currently spin up a separate process for each multiplayer document which everyone editing that document connects to." Conflicts resolve per property, last writer wins, with the server authoritative ("Figma's multiplayer servers reject parent property updates that would cause a cycle"); they rejected operational transforms as "unnecessarily complex for our problem space"; offline edits are reapplied on a fresh copy at reconnect; undo "modifies redo history at the time of the undo" (https://www.figma.com/blog/how-figmas-multiplayer-technology-works/). A slide deck is closer to Figma's object graph than to a text document: most mutations here are already last writer wins by server order (`transform.ts` line 7) and only text runs are transformed.

Notion: shipped an RGA based sequence CRDT with Peritext style rich text annotations to production in July 2025, "one of the largest CRDT deployments in the world", processing millions of operations a minute; operations flow through Notion's servers, which load the blocks and merge (https://www.notion.com/blog/how-notion-handles-concurrent-editing-with-crdts). A CRDT did not make Notion peer to peer; the server stays the store and the relay.

Linear: a server written, ordered sync action log; "Each sync action has an ordered ID, the affected model, the type of change, routing metadata, and the data a client must apply. These sync actions form an application-level log that clients replay into their local databases." Postgres commits the authoritative log; a serving index answers reconnects (https://linear.app/now/rebuilding-delta-sync-read-path). Linear uses server assigned ids with last write wins, not OT or a CRDT (https://performance.dev/what-makes-linear-feel-instant).

All four put one authority between the browsers. Turboslide's reasons are the same: share links with a Viewer role need a point that refuses writes, the agent writes over HTTP and needs a place to land, the version log needs one writer that assigns numbers, and undo per author needs an ordered history to transform against.

## 5. The options

### 5a. A server ordered log with a pub/sub fan out (Upstash Redis, SSE or WebSockets from Vercel functions)

Mechanism: the existing redis tier. A Vercel function admits the POST and runs the Lua compare and append (`lua.ts` `APPEND_SCRIPT`), which appends to the deck's stream and publishes once; every instance with an open stream for the deck holds one subscription and pushes the event to its tabs; presence is a hash and a sorted set with `SET PX` bindings (`redis.ts` lines 1 to 6). Carets would travel as presence state over the same channel, which is what `BLOB_TIER_NOTICE` already promises. Vercel Functions now serve WebSocket connections natively, in public beta since 2026-06-22, "billing only applies to the time your Function spends processing messages, not idle connection time" for Active CPU (https://vercel.com/changelog/websocket-support-is-now-in-public-beta; https://vercel.com/kb/guide/do-vercel-serverless-functions-support-websocket-connections), so the per tab channel can be SSE as today or a WebSocket.

Latency, estimate: a keystroke is coalesced 100 ms on the client (`room-client.ts` line 6), one HTTP POST to the function's region (10 to 40 ms in region), one Lua call to Upstash in the same region (1 to 5 ms), one pub/sub delivery to the subscribing instance (a few ms), one push down the stream (10 to 40 ms). About 150 to 250 ms keystroke to the other screen in one region, carets the same path at the 80 ms presence batch. Across regions the function runs in one region by default (`iad1`; Pro may run up to 3, https://vercel.com/docs/functions/limitations), so a browser in Europe adds 80 to 100 ms on each leg: 300 to 450 ms. This is the floor of a function hosted channel: every message takes two hops through a function and a database, never one.

Cost, section 6 load, prices read 2026-10-01. Upstash: about 5,000 commands per editor hour (one Lua call per op, a set and a publish per presence message, the subscriber deliveries): 7.5 M commands a month at 50 editor hours a day, 75 M at 500; pay as you go is $0.20 per 100K commands ($15 and $150), a fixed 250 MB plan is $10 a month and a 1 GB plan $20 with 10,000 commands a second and 50 or 100 GB of bandwidth, enough for the 22 GB a month of the 500 hour case (https://upstash.com/pricing/redis; https://upstash.com/docs/redis/overall/pricing). Upstash does not publish a concurrent connection limit on either page; the error "ERR max concurrent connections exceeded" exists (https://upstash.com/docs/redis/troubleshooting/max_concurrent_connections), and every fluid compute instance holds one subscriber connection, so the limit must be asked for before the tier runs on production. Vercel: provisioned memory is billed for the whole life of an instance including I/O waits (https://vercel.com/docs/functions/usage-and-pricing), so a tab's open stream or WebSocket costs 2 GB × $0.0106 per GB hour at `iad1` = $0.021 per tab hour when it holds an instance alone, shared when fluid concurrency packs tabs on one instance: $32 a month at 50 editor hours a day and $318 at 500, held alone; invocations at $0.60 a million are $2 and $21 for the ops and presence POSTs, and fall near zero over a WebSocket. Total for the realtime part: about $45 a month at 50 editor hours and $360 at 500. The blob tier's own realtime share today is the pulse, presence and manifest reads of `docs/SYNC.md` 4.3 ($18.32 and $181.97 of Blob operations) plus the same stream memory, so this option removes the Blob read line and adds $10 to $20 of Redis.

Codebase: the smallest change of the four. `select.ts` already chooses redis from `REDIS_URL`; the version log stays `versions/<n>.json` on Blob through the checkpointer (`room.ts` `roomBackedStore`, line 1126); the agent's HTTP write stays as it is; the access cache can drop its 5 s TTL by the message another instance publishes (`packages/store/src/access-store.ts` line 8 names that bus). Work to do: carets in the presence state and their rendering, the redis tier verified on a preview with `REDIS_URL` (the people round's rule, `docs/PEOPLE.md` 2.2 default 1), the connection limit answer from Upstash, and the stream's memory cost kept in view.

Lock in: Redis commands and three Lua scripts; any Redis (Upstash, Redis Cloud, a Vercel Marketplace Redis) runs them. Failure modes: an instance whose subscriber connection drops misses events until the client's 8 s gap watch reopens; the compare and append under load serialises through one Lua script per deck (fine at a deck's scale, the script answers heads and counts and never copies entries, `lua.ts` line 6); a region outage of the Redis database stops every write (the blob tier keeps working without Redis, so `select.ts` could fall back); the function duration cap (300 s default, 800 s maximum on Pro, https://vercel.com/docs/functions/limitations) ends every stream on a timer, as today's `STREAM_LIFETIME_MS` does.

### 5b. A stateful realtime host beside Vercel

#### Cloudflare Durable Objects, or PartyKit on your own Cloudflare account

Mechanism: one object per deck, every tab a WebSocket to it, the room's state (the live document, the pending window, the roster) in the object's memory and its SQLite storage. The admission code of `room.ts` (`admitOps`, line 1693, the memory tier's path) runs inside the object unchanged in shape: it is one process per deck, which is the memory tier's assumption. The object commits version records to Blob the way the checkpointer does today, so `versions/<n>.json`, `deck.trash {baseRevision}` and the agent surface keep their meaning; the Vercel app keeps the pages, the loaders, the exports and the agent HTTP routes, and forwards an agent write to the deck's object. PartyKit is this with a nicer API: it joined Cloudflare on 2024-04-05 and "you can now use PartyKit for free" by deploying to your own Cloudflare account (https://blog.partykit.io/posts/partykit-is-joining-cloudflare). Hibernation keeps sockets open while the object is out of memory, "Clients remain connected while the Durable Object is not in memory", and "Billable Duration (GB-s) charges do not accrue during hibernation" (https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/best-practices/websockets/); PartyKit allows 100 connections a room without hibernation and 32,000 with it, and notes hibernation is "Incompatible with Yjs-based applications currently" (https://docs.partykit.io/guides/scaling-partykit-servers-with-hibernation/). An object has a soft limit of 1,000 requests a second and 10 GB of SQLite storage on the paid plan (https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/limits/).

Latency, estimate: the object is created near the first tab that opens the deck. In one region: the 100 ms coalesce, one WebSocket frame to the object (10 to 30 ms), the admission in memory (under 5 ms), one frame to the other tab (10 to 30 ms): 120 to 170 ms keystroke to screen, and carets at 20 to 60 ms with no batch needed. Across regions the far browser pays its round trip to the object's colo once per direction: 200 to 300 ms. This is the lowest floor of the server options because there is one hop and no database on the path.

Cost, prices read 2026-10-01 (https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/pricing/): Workers Paid $5 a month minimum; requests 1 M a month included then $0.15 a million with WebSocket messages billed at 20:1; duration 400,000 GB-s a month included then $12.50 a million GB-s; SQLite rows 25 billion reads and 50 million writes included. With a 128 MB object awake only while someone edits (the heartbeat answered by the runtime's auto response so it does not wake the object): 90 GB-s an editor hour, 135,000 GB-s a month at 50 editor hours a day (included) and 1.35 M at 500 ($12); awake the whole hour instead: $3 and $79. Messages: 4,400 an editor hour at 20:1 plus the HTTP calls, under the included million at 50 hours and about $1 at 500. Total: $5 to $9 a month at 50 editor hours and $18 to $85 at 500, and the Vercel stream memory and presence invocations of 5a disappear. Storage of the version records stays on Blob as today.

Codebase: a second deploy target (`wrangler`), a Worker that terminates the WebSocket and routes `/api/decks/:id/stream`, `/ops` and `/presence` to the object, the admission and the roster moved into the object, a bearer path from the Vercel routes to the object, and the editor's room client pointed at the object's host. `packages/realtime` is already structured for this: the memory channel (`packages/realtime/src/memory.ts` line 47) is the object's channel. The authorization decision (`decideFor`, `room.ts` line 716) must run in the object or be passed to it as a signed grant.

Lock in: the Durable Object storage and hibernation APIs, which only Cloudflare runs; the room logic is TypeScript and ports to any single process host (a Fly machine, a Node server). Failure modes: an object is evicted and reconstructed, so every piece of state that matters must be in SQLite or on Blob before the answer goes out; one object is one thread, so one very busy deck is capped at the soft limit; a Cloudflare incident takes the rooms down while the Vercel pages stay up; two deploy surfaces to keep in step, and the GT team's Vercel domain guard (`~/.config/turboslide/gt-follow.sh`) has no equivalent on the Worker.

#### Liveblocks

Mechanism: hosted rooms with presence, their own storage types (LiveObject, LiveList) or Yjs, and webhooks for persistence. Pricing (https://liveblocks.io/pricing): Free $0 with 3,000 collaboration minutes a month; Pro $30 a month with $30 of credit, then $0.002 a collaboration minute, 10 simultaneous connections a room, 10 MB of storage a room. Cost: 90,000 collaboration minutes a month at 50 editor hours a day is $180; 900,000 at 500 is $1,800. Latency: their edge, one hop, comparable to 5b's first option. Codebase: the version log would be rebuilt from their storage through webhooks, the agent's HTTP write would go through their REST API, and `admitOps`' reducer and validator would run only on the client. Lock in: the storage model is theirs. Failure mode: the 10 connections a room cap on Pro, and the per minute price grows with every open tab.

#### Ably or Pusher

Mechanism: a channel service for the fan out only. The ordering point stays ours (Redis or Blob): the Vercel function admits and then publishes; tabs subscribe to the deck's channel and presence set directly, so no function instance is held open. Ably (https://ably.com/pricing): free 6 M messages a month and 200 peak connections; Standard $29 a month plus $2.50 a million messages, $1 a million connection minutes and $1 a million channel minutes; 2,500 messages a second and 10,000 connections on Standard. Cost: about $46 a month at 50 editor hours (6.6 M messages) and $196 at 500 (66 M). Pusher (https://pusher.com/channels/pricing/): Sandbox free at 200,000 messages a day and 100 connections; Startup $49 for 1 M a day and 500 connections; Pro $99 for 4 M a day and 2,000 connections. Cost: $49 at 50 editor hours (220,000 messages a day) and $99 at 500 (2.2 M a day). Latency: the admission hop of 5a plus the service's edge delivery, 150 to 250 ms in region, and the service is multi region so the cross region penalty is the function's alone. Codebase: a publisher behind `RealtimeChannel` and the client's stream replaced by the vendor SDK; the agent and the version log unchanged. Lock in: the SDK and the channel names. Failure modes: two systems on the write path (the order point and the fan out) that can disagree during an outage, and a message delivered before its record is readable.

#### Supabase Realtime

Mechanism: Broadcast and Presence over Phoenix channels, transport only like Ably. Quotas (https://supabase.com/docs/guides/realtime/quotas): 200 concurrent connections and 100 messages a second on Free, 500 and 500 on Pro, 10,000 and 2,500 on Pro without a spend cap. Pricing (https://supabase.com/pricing): Pro includes 500 peak connections and 5 M messages a month, then $10 a thousand connections and $2.50 a million messages. Cost: the Pro subscription plus $4 a month at 50 editor hours and plus $152 at 500. Latency and codebase as Ably. Failure mode: the 500 messages a second cap on Pro with a spend cap is reachable by a few busy rooms; the project is a database product and the realtime part is sized to it.

### 5c. A CRDT (Yjs or Automerge) with a server for persistence and awareness

Mechanism: the document becomes shared types (Y.Map per slide, Y.Text per text run); clients merge updates locally with no transform and the server relays and stores binary updates. "Yjs doesn't make any assumptions about the network technology you are using. As long as all changes eventually arrive, the documents will sync" and it "doesn't need a central source of truth" (https://docs.yjs.dev/). Awareness carries carets and names as ephemeral state that "isn't stored in the Yjs document" (https://docs.yjs.dev/getting-started/adding-awareness). Servers: Hocuspocus (Node WebSocket, hooks `onAuthenticate`, `onLoadDocument`, `onStoreDocument` debounced, `onChange` "can be fired up to multiple times a second", a Redis extension for horizontal scaling; https://tiptap.dev/docs/hocuspocus/introduction; https://tiptap.dev/docs/hocuspocus/server/hooks), y-partykit on a Durable Object (snapshot or history persistence with a 10 MB history cap, a `callback` "called every few seconds after edits" to write elsewhere; https://docs.partykit.io/reference/y-partykit-api/), or Liveblocks Yjs. Automerge is the alternative CRDT with a full change history and automerge-repo adapters for WebSocket and storage (https://automerge.org/docs/hello/).

Latency: the host's (5b): local apply at once, one hop to the other tab, 120 to 170 ms in region with the coalesce, carets at 20 to 60 ms. Cost: the host's; a Yjs room cannot hibernate on Durable Objects, so the awake the whole hour figures apply: $8 a month at 50 editor hours and $84 at 500 on your own Cloudflare account; or Liveblocks' $180 and $1,800.

What a CRDT changes here. Undo: Y.UndoManager undoes only the tracked origins' changes and merges edits within a 500 ms `captureTimeout` (https://docs.yjs.dev/api/undo-manager), which replaces `transformSince` and `undoOfSplices` (`room-client.ts` line 410; `room.ts` line 1584) and is a known good per author undo. Server authority: the server stores bytes it does not interpret, so `admitOps`' reducer and validator (`room.ts` line 1693), the 200 kB slide and 25 MB deck caps (`admission.ts` lines 35 to 37) and the Viewer role become connection level checks or client side rules; a merged update that breaks the deck schema has no place to be refused. The version log: `versions/<n>.json` with its `origin.opIds` and the `Mutation` entries would become snapshots on a timer; `deck.trash {baseRevision}` and `requiredRevisionId` style refusals need a server sequence the CRDT does not give. The agent surface: every action in `apps/studio/src/server/actions.ts` produces `Mutation`s; a server side Y.Doc would have to apply them and translate them to Yjs operations, and the agent's "reload and rebase" answer disappears because a CRDT never refuses. The whole `packages/schema` reducer and the text runs would be rewritten over Y types. Gain: character exact merges with no lost word in any order (`sync.title.concurrent-both-kept` is solved by construction), offline merges, carets for free. Lock in: the document format is the library's binary update log; Yjs and Automerge do not interoperate. Failure modes: unbounded update history until compaction, a schema change that old clients merge wrongly, and the same single room process failure modes as 5b.

### 5d. True peer to peer over WebRTC (y-webrtc)

Mechanism: tabs connect to each other in a mesh and exchange CRDT updates and awareness directly. y-webrtc still needs a signaling server ("The peers find each other by connecting to a signaling server"; the defaults are public servers), caps a browser at 20 plus a random 0 to 14 connections, encrypts with a room password, and is "Not suited for a large amount of collaborators on a single document (each peer is connected to each other)" (https://github.com/yjs/y-webrtc). It has no persistence: when the last tab closes the deck is wherever a tab saved it, so a persistence peer that is always on is needed, and that peer is a server. NAT: direct connections fail for a share of sessions and need a TURN relay, "particularly in scenarios where direct communication is obstructed by NATs or firewalls" (https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/); secondary sources put the relayed share at 10 to 25 percent of sessions, higher on carrier grade and corporate symmetric NAT (https://bloggeek.me/webrtcglossary/turn/; https://www.nojitter.com/know-where-turn-when-deploying-webrtc). TURN pricing: Cloudflare $0.05 a GB outbound (https://developers.cloudflare.com/realtime/turn/), Twilio $0.40 to $0.80 a GB by region (https://www.twilio.com/en-us/stun-turn/pricing).

Latency, estimate: the best of the four when the direct path works, 20 to 60 ms between two browsers in one region and 100 to 200 ms across regions, one hop and no server; through a TURN relay one more hop. Carets the same.

Cost: the traffic is tiny. 33 M messages a month at about 300 bytes at 500 editor hours a day is 10 GB; a quarter relayed is 2.5 GB, $0.13 on Cloudflare or $1 to $2 on Twilio; the signaling server is a Worker or a small function. The persistence peer is a Durable Object or a Node process at 5b's price. Money is not the objection.

What it would mean here: everything 5c changes, plus the loss of the authority. A Viewer share link cannot be enforced between peers: a peer that holds the room key can send any update, and a read only role would need signed updates and every peer verifying them. The agent's HTTP write has no peer to land on except the persistence peer, which then is the server the design was meant to remove. The version log has no single writer, so revision numbers have no order and `deck.trash {baseRevision}` has no meaning. The 5 s link index problem moves to the signaling layer. Lock in: WebRTC and the CRDT format. Failure modes: a mesh partition that merges late and surprises a seller in a meeting; a corporate network that blocks UDP and the relay; a browser tab as the only holder of ten minutes of edits; a public signaling server as a dependency. This is why none of the four products in sections 3 and 4 does it: Google keeps "a single state space" on the server, Figma runs "a separate process for each multiplayer document", Notion's operations flow through its servers, Linear's log is server ordered.

## 6. The load model behind the cost figures

One editor hour is `docs/SYNC.md` 4.3's: 12 minutes of editing at one edit per 5 s and 48 idle minutes, in a room of two on average. Per editor hour: 144 ops; about 1,440 caret and selection messages while editing at 2 a second (the batch is 80 ms and the cap 15 a second, `protocol.ts` lines 54 and 55, so this is a low estimate for a fast typist); 576 heartbeats at 5 s while idle (`protocol.ts` line 60). About 2,200 messages sent and the same number delivered to the other tab: 4,400 billable messages where a vendor counts both directions, 60 connection minutes, 300 bytes a message. Months are 30 days: 1,500 editor hours and 6.6 M billable messages at 50 editor hours a day; 15,000 hours and 66 M messages at 500. Vercel memory uses the 2 GB default instance at `iad1` ($0.0106 a GB hour, https://vercel.com/docs/functions/usage-and-pricing). Figures are for the realtime part alone; the pages, the exports and the Blob storage are the same under every option.

## 8. Recommendation

Should it be peer to peer: no. Peer to peer saves no money here (the whole month's traffic relays for under $5), and it removes the three things a seller's deck needs a single place for: a Viewer share link that refuses writes, an agent that writes over HTTP and must land somewhere, and a version log whose numbers are ordered so `deck.trash {baseRevision}` and undo per author mean something. y-webrtc itself still needs a signaling server and a persistence peer, so the server comes back under another name, and 10 to 25 percent of sessions go through a relay anyway. Google, Figma, Notion and Linear each keep one authority between the browsers (sections 3 and 4) for these reasons, and the two defects Kevin named are not caused by the server being in the path; they are caused by the Blob store being the path: a commit is a manifest put and the other instance learns of it on a 2 s pulse and a 5 s presence record (section 2). The recommendation is option 5a, the server ordered log on Upstash Redis that is already in the tree (`redis.ts`, `lua.ts`, `select.ts`): it keeps the transform, the version log and the agent surface as they are, it is a `REDIS_URL` on `turboslide-gt` plus carets in the presence state and a preview verification, and it takes the merge from 2.6 to 4.6 s to an estimated 150 to 250 ms and the chip from 2.5 to 10 s to the presence batch, at $10 to $20 a month of Redis.

The second best option, and the destination if the Vercel memory line or the latency floor becomes the problem at 500 editor hours a day, is 5b's Durable Object (or PartyKit on the GT Cloudflare account): one object per deck running the same `room.ts` admission over WebSockets, committing the version records to Blob as today, at an estimated 120 to 170 ms in region and $18 to $85 a month at 500 editor hours against $360 for 5a. It costs a second deploy surface and a bearer path from the Vercel routes to the object, which is why it is second and not first. A CRDT (5c) is the right answer only if character exact merging in every order is worth rebuilding the schema, undo, the version log and the agent surface; the current transform already keeps both words in the measured runs, and the lost second word of `sync.title.concurrent-both-kept` round 2 is a defect of the 1,000 ms append spacing and the pulse, not of the transform.

Sign in is a separate decision from the transport. None of the four options needs Google login; a central authority can use it for the Viewer and Editor roles, Follow (which only follows accounts, `docs/POLISH.md` line 148) and the verified badge. The mailroom pattern is Auth.js with a `Google` provider and `AUTH_GOOGLE_ID` and `AUTH_GOOGLE_SECRET` (`/Users/kevinliu/repos/mailroom/src/auth.ts` lines 2 and 40 to 44; `.env.example` lines 2 to 3, redirect URI `<origin>/api/auth/callback/google`); its better-auth counterpart is one more entry beside `github` in `socialProviders` (`better-auth.ts` lines 158 to 166 and 233), and it needs `DATABASE_URL` on production first (`docs/PEOPLE.md` 2.2 default 1).
